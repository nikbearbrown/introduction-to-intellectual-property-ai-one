# 1. Inventory the contributions

*The repository tells you who uploaded the files. It does not tell you who can release them.*

Suppose three teammates build **Shelf Kite**, a fictional campus equipment checkout app. They write its interface, paste a scheduling function from a public repository, generate a banner, and demonstrate a routing method. The team wants to post the source and show the prototype at an open house. The two releases are different acts. A file that never leaves the laptop can still be revealed on a slide. A demo stripped of the slide can still redistribute the function.

This book introduces United States intellectual property for business and design students. The primary references were checked November 26, 2026. No legal background is assumed. You will classify issues, collect evidence, and write a handoff for qualified review. You will not decide who would win a dispute. The project, brand and records are invented for teaching; the name is not a clearance result.

## What a good register records

A useful register has an item, its exact version, how it arrived, who did what, evidence of authority, and a decision about a **specific** use. Do not fill a blank with a plausible guess. Record what would resolve it.

| Item | Evidence observed | Question for the release |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interface at build 7 | Team design drafts and commits | Do contributions and any agreements support distribution in the public build? |
| Scheduling function | Public Git repository at a recorded revision; no permission located | What terms, if any, govern copying this part into a public repository? |
| Banner | Generation history and original layout draft | Which elements were supplied, generated, or independently edited? Are the inputs authorized? |
| Proposed name | One similar name in a public search | Who uses the other name, where, and for which goods or services? |
| Routing method | Restricted notes and a proposed public demo | Does the demo disclose details not yet reviewed for release? |

A commit author is a clue to origin, not proof that the named account created every line, owns it, or had authority to grant permission. Save the revision, relevant path and record date. For documents, save the version or file fingerprint and the evidence location. Preserve sensitive evidence inside the authorized workspace, not in the public report.

## Read a contribution as a chain

Start with the concrete object. Who made it, under what relationship, and with whose inputs? What happened next: a grant, assignment, upload, edit, or disclosure? Then name what you propose to do. Viewing, copying, editing, distributing a source archive and putting a design on a product page can raise different questions.

Take the banner. The pictured content is not the same thing as the original layout. If you collapse the two into one row, you turn partial evidence about one component into apparent clearance for both. Split the row the moment the evidence differs. Preserve the clean layout as a separately reviewable asset.

**Try it unaided.** A teammate says a zip is “ours” because its account owner uploaded it. List three independent records that would make that statement more useful. Then identify one right or permission none of your records alone would establish. In the next chapter, use the register to decide which questions those records answer.
