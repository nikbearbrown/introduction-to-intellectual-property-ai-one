# 2. Compare the protections

*Four different questions can hide inside one launch.*

Open the Shelf Kite homepage. Its banner is visual expression. Its name identifies a service. Its booking system may implement a technical method. Its nonpublic routing rules may provide an advantage. These are different mechanisms, not four labels for owning the whole app. One item can raise several questions at once.

| Mechanism | What it is about | Question to ask first |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copyright | Original expression fixed in a medium, not the underlying ideas, methods or facts as such | Which expression was copied, and what authority supports the proposed use? |
| Patent | An exclusionary right defined by the claims of a granted patent | Is there a potentially relevant claim, who contributed to its conception, and what has been disclosed? |
| Trademark | A sign that identifies the source of goods or services | Would the use risk confusion in the relevant commercial context? |
| Trade secret | Qualifying information that has value from not being generally known or readily ascertainable and is subject to reasonable secrecy measures | What is actually secret, who knows it, and what limits access? |

## Separate the moving parts

A copyright can cover a particular piece of code without conferring ownership of the computation it performs. Replacing the code with an independently written implementation can change the copying question; it does not by itself resolve a possible patent claim. Federal copyright law states the expression/idea distinction in [17 U.S.C. § 102](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html#102). The USPTO describes the different exclusions, examples and administrative roles in its [comparison of patent, trademark, and copyright](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/trademark-patent-copyright).

A federal trademark search is a good starting point, not a certification. Suppose the team finds “Shelf Flight” used for shelving hardware and “Shelf Kites” used for facility reservations. Similarity of names and relatedness of goods or services both matter; neither a shared word nor a different spelling settles confusion. Search variations, record the relevant goods and note what the search does not cover. The point is to make the next review reproducible, not to declare one of these fictional names safe. See the [USPTO’s clearance-search guide](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/comprehensive-clearance-search-similar-trademarks).

Trade secrecy is not a label you apply retroactively to something you already published. Under [18 U.S.C. § 1839](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t18/s1839/), the inquiry includes economically valuable secrecy and reasonable measures to preserve it. Restricted repositories, need-based access and an agreed confidentiality obligation can supply evidence about those measures; none alone proves every element. Unlike a patent, secrecy protection is not a right to stop someone from independently deriving the same information through proper means.

## Check the two kinds of human work

A form that asks for a copyright author is not asking for an inventor. The Copyright Office’s [AI report, Part 2](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/) distinguishes protectable human expression from material generated by a system and considers human selection, arrangement or modifications case by case. Record the human layout and changes rather than assigning one status to a whole screen. For patents, use the [USPTO’s revised November 2025 guidance](https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2025/revised-inventorship-guidance-ai-assisted-inventions), which superseded the February 2024 guidance. Ordinary inventorship standards apply to AI-assisted work; an AI system is not a named inventor. Document who contributed to conception rather than listing everyone who typed a prompt or ran a test.

**Try it unaided.** For the routing notes, name one plausible patent question and one separate secrecy question. Give an observation that would change one answer while leaving the other unresolved.
