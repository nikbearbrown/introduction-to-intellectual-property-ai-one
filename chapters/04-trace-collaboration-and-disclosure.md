# 4. Trace collaboration and disclosure

*The same information has a different release history on a restricted drive and on a livestream.*

In the fictional Shelf Kite project, a student wrote the prototype routing rule on Monday, a contractor tuned it on Wednesday, and an AI assistant suggested a test on Friday. One week later a slide draft shows the threshold and the routing rule. The team logs four events, not a single “the team built it” entry. The people, artifacts and dates answer different questions.

## Build the timeline before debating ownership

Record who contributed, what they contributed, their role at that time, and how you know. Keep copyright ownership, patent inventorship and an agreement governing access in separate columns. [17 U.S.C. § 201](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html#201) starts with authorship and contains rules for works made for hire. The label “contractor” alone does not settle ownership or identify a work made for hire; look for the facts and agreements and refer unresolved cases. A payment receipt is not automatically a transfer. Verify who is authorized to sign off, rather than asking a teammate to guess.

For potential patent inventorship, document the actual contribution to conception, what is claimed or being considered, and when. Do not score inventorship by number of commits. A genuine contributor can be missed if only the repository owner is listed. Keep the ownership inquiry separate from identification of potential inventors.

## Ask what each audience would learn

| Event | What the audience can inspect | Evidence to recover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Restricted dry run | Live result plus full routing values | Attendee list, permissions and any confidentiality terms |
| Proposed open house | Slides and screen actions | Final slides, public stream/replay plan, and exact content exposed |
| Public demo recording already posted | Potentially durable copy | Exact timestamp, clip/version, access history and what was visible |

For a U.S. patent, a public disclosure can matter to novelty. [35 U.S.C. § 102](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t35/s102/) includes exceptions for specified facts and timing, not a universal year of safety. Other jurisdictions can apply different rules. If a disclosure already happened, log its date, content, audience and access without treating later removal as an undo button. Ask about the actual venues and release schedule before assuming a domestic answer covers the intended markets.

A reduced demo might convey the experience without showing unpublished thresholds. That only helps if the remaining footage, files and interface reveal no details requiring review. Compare an exact demo build against the release candidate, and keep both evidence trails. “NDA” is not a universal answer; the secrecy inquiry includes actual handling and distribution.

**Try it unaided.** Compare a private test to the open house. What fact about each event would make the label misleading? Draft a stop condition the operator can use without deciding a legal doctrine.
